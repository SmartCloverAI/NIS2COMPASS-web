# Recognise And Respond

Phishing is a deceptive request intended to get money, information or access from you. You can ask for help without deciding whether the request was definitely fraudulent.

## Take The Next Safe Step

Stop interacting with the suspect message, page or caller. Use the section below that matches what happened; more than one may apply. If money is involved, contact the bank promptly as well as reporting any work-related incident.

> For work equipment, accounts or information, follow the organisation's incident process and contact its known technical-support or security team. Do not install cleanup tools, investigate the device or change safety-critical equipment yourself. Keep essential work safe and follow the responsible team's instructions about disconnection or shutdown.

Reach help through an established workplace directory, official app, known bookmark or existing contact record. Do not use the suspect request's reply address, phone number or offered call transfer. A search advertisement is not a substitute for a trusted recovery route.

## You Only Received The Request

Leave the requested action unfinished. If it might concern a genuine task, verify it through a separate trusted contact before proceeding. Report it through the workplace reporting channel, even if you did not click. For a personal account, use the service's established reporting option or locally applicable official guidance.

A report lets the responsible team assess the request and warn others if needed. Keep the original available according to that channel's instructions; you do not need to open an attachment or test a link to provide evidence. Your uncertainty is useful information.

## You Clicked But Did Nothing Else

Stop using the page and report that you opened it. A click does not prove infection, and not typing does not establish safety. Tell workplace support what happened. On a personal device, follow its provider's instructions and use existing trusted security software for an appropriate scan, rather than a cleanup offer on the page. The [UK National Cyber Security Centre's response guidance](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/what-to-do) covers opened links and workplace help.

If you also downloaded something, supplied information or approved access, follow the relevant section below. Do not reopen the site to check what it does.

## You Shared A Password Or Code, Or Approved Access

Treat this as possible account access, even if the service still works. Contact workplace support for a work account. For a personal account, use the provider's trusted recovery route from a device you trust. Change a disclosed password and address other accounts using the same password. Tell support if you shared a code or approved a sign-in; a password change may not be the only action needed.

A signed-in session is access that remains active after login, such as an app that does not ask for your password each time. Follow the provider's instructions to end existing sessions, remove any unwanted permissions given to apps, and review changes to email forwarding and recovery details. If you are locked out, use account recovery instead of repeatedly trying the suspect link. These steps address access that could remain after a password change. See the [National Cyber Security Centre's account-recovery guidance](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/recovering-a-hacked-account).

## You Downloaded, Opened Or Installed Something

Do not open a downloaded file or reopen one you already opened. Do not run further instructions. Tell work support which of these happened: download only, opening a file, installing software or running a command. If unsure, say so. That distinction helps the team decide what to check without asking you to investigate.

On a personal device, use the existing trusted security tools and the device provider's instructions for checking suspected malware, meaning harmful software. Do not download an unfamiliar cleaner from a warning or caller. Keep security software updated and seek provider support if you cannot complete its checks. The [Federal Trade Commission's phishing guidance](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams) covers security software and responses to harmful downloads.

## You Paid Or Shared Bank Details

Contact your bank or payment provider promptly through its official app or an existing trusted contact, and explain what you paid or disclosed. Ask what protective steps are available. For work payments, also notify the responsible finance and incident contacts. Follow local official instructions for reporting suspected crime. The [National Cyber Security Centre recommends bank contact after financial disclosure or loss](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/what-to-do). Recovery or a refund cannot be guaranteed.

## You Shared A Sensitive Document

Sensitive information includes personal records and confidential files. Stop further sharing and report the disclosure to the organisation's designated incident or privacy contact and the document owner. Describe the type of information, where you sent it and whether you used a sharing link. Do not spread another copy in a group chat to explain the problem.

For personal documents, use the relevant service's trusted support instructions for the exposure. Ask about controlling further access; do not assume deleting your message retrieves someone else's copy. Prompt reporting lets the responsible people consider protection for those whose information was involved.

## What A Useful Report Contains

Give the approximate time, the channel used and the sender as displayed. Explain the requested action and what you actually did, including any payment, approval or disclosure. State which account or device was involved, without giving its password. Include uncertainty rather than filling gaps with guesses.

Use the approved reporting function or follow the team's instructions for supplying the original message. Do not send passwords, authentication codes or sensitive attachments to prove what happened. A near-miss report can help identify related requests; a prompt report after a mistake gives responders more useful context than waiting for certainty.

## Protective Habits

Keep the approved reporting route and important service contacts available before you need them. Practise the independent checks in [Everyday Examples](everyday-examples.md).

Use supported passkeys where available. A passkey is a sign-in method tied to the genuine service, which helps resist fake login sites. Where passkeys are unavailable, use a unique password and multi-factor authentication, or MFA: more than one kind of proof at sign-in. Codes and approval prompts can still be phished. Protect your device and recovery options; neither method verifies a payment or document request. The [National Cyber Security Centre's passkey guidance](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/passkeys-are-more-secure-than-traditional-ways-to-log-in) explains the distinction.
