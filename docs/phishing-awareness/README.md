# Phishing Awareness

Phishing is a deception that asks you to do something for someone pretending to be trustworthy. That might mean signing in, approving access, paying an invoice or sharing a document. Impersonation means pretending to be another person or organisation. The useful first question is what the sender wants you to do, before deciding whether the message looks convincing.

> Already clicked, approved a sign-in, paid or shared something? Go to [Recognise And Respond](recognise-and-respond.md) for immediate steps. Follow your organisation's incident process for work accounts and equipment.

## A Familiar Situation

You are waiting for a parcel. A text message says a delivery problem needs your attention and offers a link. This is a fictional example, with no working destination.

The message asks you to leave the conversation and act somewhere else. Your expected delivery makes the story plausible, but does not establish who sent it. Open the delivery service's official app or use tracking information from your original order instead. That lets you check the parcel without trusting the route supplied by the unknown sender. A small requested payment deserves the same check as a large one.

The same reasoning applies to an unexpected account warning or a colleague's request for a file: identify the action and find a separate way to confirm it.

## What Someone Could Gain

A stolen password can give someone access to an account. A false payment request can send money to the wrong recipient. Sensitive information means material that needs protection, such as identity documents, financial details or confidential work files. Once shared with the wrong person, it may be difficult to recover. Some requests lead to malware, software designed to cause harm or steal information. The [US Federal Trade Commission's phishing guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-recognize-avoid-phishing-scams) explains these account and information risks.

At home, consider the inconvenience of losing access to the email account you use for bookings and bills. At work, consider an invoice awaiting approval or a folder containing customer records. The decision may feel small because it is part of a routine; the account, money or information involved can still matter to other people.

You do not have to hold a senior job or valuable secrets to receive a deceptive request. Ordinary tasks provide believable reasons to ask for action. Being helpful, finishing a busy shift or avoiding a missed delivery are reasonable concerns. Practice should make room for those pressures, without blaming the person who encountered the message.

## More Than Email

Phishing can arrive as an email, an SMS text message, a chat message or a phone call. A QR code, the square pattern a phone can scan to open a destination, can also direct you to a website. A request can move between channels: a caller might ask you to approve something on your phone while continuing the conversation.

Focus on the action across those channels. Does the person want money, information, account access or software installed? A change of channel does not settle whether they have authority to ask.

## Clues Help You Pause

An unusual deadline, pressure to bypass a normal check or a changed payment destination deserves attention. Poor spelling can be a clue, but polished writing, a familiar display name and a logo do not establish who is behind a request. The [UK National Cyber Security Centre's guidance on spotting scams](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/spot-scams) explains why appearance alone is unreliable.

An email address identifies a sending account; a web address, also called a URL, identifies a destination. The domain is the service's address name. These details can help you notice a mismatch, but you need not become an address expert before asking for help. An encrypted connection protects traffic to a site; it does not independently prove that the site or its request is trustworthy.

## Verify Away From The Request

Use a known bookmark, the official app or a contact record you already trust. For work, use the locally approved route. Do not rely on a reply to the suspect message, its new phone number, an unsolicited call transfer or the first search advertisement.

Separate contact gives the real service or colleague a chance to confirm the request. If you cannot confirm it, leave the action unfinished and report the uncertainty. You do not need proof of fraud. Reporting a near miss can help the responsible team recognise related requests; reporting an accidental interaction gives them a chance to respond sooner.

## Three Measures Of The Problem

- In the [UK government's 2025/2026 business survey](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026/cyber-security-breaches-survey-20252026), 38% said they identified phishing in the 12 months before interview. This includes attempts.
- [Anti-Phishing Working Group (APWG)](https://docs.apwg.org/reports/apwg_trends_report_q2_2026.pdf) recorded about 1.07 million reported phishing-site attacks during April-June 2026 through its international reporting network, not that many victims.
- The [United States Federal Bureau of Investigation's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) recorded about US$216 million in reported losses in its 2025 phishing/spoofing complaint category, including international submissions. Spoofing means disguising an identity or source.

[Phishing In Numbers](phishing-in-numbers.md) explains the populations and limits. These figures cannot predict your personal likelihood of being deceived.
