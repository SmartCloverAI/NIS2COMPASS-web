# Everyday Examples

Phishing uses a believable identity or situation to persuade you to take an unsafe action. The situations below are fictional. They contain no working links, codes, attachments or real participant information. Each leaves something unverified; deciding what to check is more useful than guessing whether the writing looks suspicious.

For a real incident, use [Recognise And Respond](recognise-and-respond.md). Work accounts and equipment follow the organisation's incident process. Do not interrupt safety-critical work or change equipment settings to investigate a message; contact the responsible team through the established route.

## A Delivery Text Arrives During Your Shift

You ordered a household item yesterday. An SMS, or text message, arrives while you are leaving for work. It says a delivery detail needs correcting and asks you to follow a link and pay a small charge. You would like to settle it before the bus arrives.

The requested actions are opening a new destination and supplying payment information. The message has not established that it belongs to your order or that the charge is genuine. An expected parcel explains why you would pay attention; it does not identify the sender.

Leave the link alone. Check the order through the shop's existing app or your original order record, using a route you already trust. If help is needed, contact the service there. You can resolve a genuine delivery problem without accepting the text's account of it.

<details><summary>Does a small charge make it reasonable to skip verification?</summary><p>No. The amount does not establish who would receive your payment information. Checking the original order gives you a separate basis for deciding whether anything needs paying.</p></details>

## An Account Warning And An Unexpected Approval

You are reading a shared work document when a message says your account needs attention. Soon afterwards, your phone asks you to approve a sign-in. You did not start one. The request feels connected to the document, but that connection is only an assumption.

The action is to approve access, not merely acknowledge a notification. You have not established who started the sign-in. Reject an unexpected request and report it through your known workplace support route. For a personal account, open the service's official app or a known bookmark and follow its security guidance. If you already approved it, use the account-access steps in [Recognise And Respond](recognise-and-respond.md).

The [Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's social-engineering guidance](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/social-engineering-itsap00166) covers deceptive consent requests and unexpected authentication prompts. Authentication means checking who is trying to sign in.

<details><summary>Would approving the prompt help you find out what the warning means?</summary><p>No. Approval can grant access before you have resolved the uncertainty. Use the established support route to check the warning without granting the requested access.</p></details>

## A Supplier Changes The Payment Contact

A reply appears in a conversation about an invoice. It names the supplier you expected and says a different colleague now handles payments. You are asked to use new contact details to confirm a changed bank account before the afternoon payment run.

Both the destination for payment and the route for checking it have changed. The existing conversation makes the request familiar, but does not verify the change. Accounts and conversation threads can be compromised, as described in the [Canadian Centre for Cyber Security guidance](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/social-engineering-itsap00166).

Hold the change and follow the organisation's supplier-verification process. Use a supplier contact already in the approved record, not the contact supplied in this message. Tell the payment team about the unresolved request. This keeps a genuine invoice moving through the correct checks without giving the unverified sender control over the confirmation.

<details><summary>Is calling the new number an independent check?</summary><p>No. The same request supplied that number, so the call could reach the same unverified person. Use the existing approved contact record and the normal payment-change process.</p></details>

## A QR Code Beside A Familiar Sign

At a venue, a printed notice asks you to scan a QR code to view a document. A QR code is a square pattern that can direct your phone to a web address. After scanning, you see a request to sign in to a different service.

The requested action has changed from reading a document to giving account access. You have not verified who placed the code or why that account is needed. The pattern itself is not malware, meaning harmful software; the question is where it leads and what happens there. The [Federal Trade Commission's QR-code advice](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/12/scammers-hide-harmful-links-qr-codes-steal-your-information) explains this destination risk.

Stop before signing in. Ask the venue through an established contact or open its known website to find the document. For a work document, use the approved document service. Checking away from the code avoids letting an unfamiliar destination decide which account you should use.

<details><summary>Does the notice's familiar logo verify the destination?</summary><p>No. The logo does not establish who placed the notice or controls the linked page. Confirm the document through the venue or service you already know.</p></details>

## A Caller Offers To Fix Your Computer

A caller introduces themselves as technical support. They know your organisation's name and say a problem needs attention before your next meeting. They ask you to install a support tool and offer to transfer you to a supervisor if you are unsure.

Installing software would give the request a consequence beyond the conversation. The caller's knowledge and proposed transfer have not established authority. A convincing voice is not proof of identity; voice impersonation is among the risks covered by the [Canadian Centre for Cyber Security](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/social-engineering-itsap00166).

End the call and contact the helpdesk through the established workplace directory. For a personal device, use the provider's support route obtained independently. Do not install the offered tool while checking. A genuine support task can be confirmed through that separate route; the caller does not need to supervise your verification.

## A Familiar Name Requests A Sensitive Document

Someone using a colleague's name asks you to upload a staff document to a new sharing location before a meeting. Sensitive information is material that needs protection, including personal records and confidential work documents. You want the colleague to have what they need on time.

The request would move information to a different place. Neither the requester's identity nor permission to receive that document has been confirmed. Contact the colleague through the existing work directory and check the authorised sharing route with the document owner. Verifying identity alone does not settle whether that file should be shared there.

This check protects the people described in the document as well as the organisation. If you already uploaded it, report the disclosure through the approved channel without attaching another copy to a broad email or chat.

## A Page Asks You To Prove You Are Human

A page that looks like a human-verification check asks you to run commands on your device. Stop. A genuine human-check puzzle does not require that. The [Federal Trade Commission's June 2026 warning](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/06/how-spot-captcha-scam) describes this deception. No command needs to be tested to decide to leave and seek trusted help.
